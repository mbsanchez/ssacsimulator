\title{From detection to action: closing the autonomic loop of a self-coordinated manufacturing line with rule-based and retrieval-augmented LLM planners}

\author[a,b]{Manuel S\'anchez\corref{cor1}}
\ead{mbsanchez@unet.edu.ve}
\author[b]{Ernesto Exposito}
\ead{ernesto.exposito@univ-pau.fr}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[a]{Universidad Nacional Experimental del T\'achira, San Crist\'obal, Venezuela}
\address[b]{Univ Pau \& Pays Adour / E2S UPPA, Laboratoire d'informatique de l'Universit\'e de Pau et des Pays de l'Adour (LIUPPA), EA3000, 64000 Pau, France}

\begin{abstract}
Autonomic architectures for Industry~4.0 promise self-healing production, but published systems often stop at detection. We revisit our earlier self-coordinated manufacturing architecture, find that it implements Monitor and Analyze but not Plan, Execute or a Knowledge base, port its simulator to a tested headless engine over the Bosch production-line dataset, and close the loop with an action space built on the line's parallel twin stations, a symbolic validator, synthetic faults and an explicit effect model. We compare rule-based planners with large-language-model (LLM) planners, with and without retrieved operational statistics, on a local 7B model (Qwen2.5) and a frontier model (Claude Fable~5.1), with parameters and prompts fixed on a development trace, on 216 fault scenarios from three held-out traces and 360 from five traces generated after the design was frozen, by the net delay recovered within 24~h of the fault. The original Analyze layer is the bottleneck: its alarms come in line-wide bursts, and with it no planner recovers more than 7\% of the delay. With a per-station change test on processing times, introduced after that failure was seen, a frontier LLM that vetoes the test's candidates recovers 41\% (95\% interval 32--50\%) on the held-out traces and 49\% (42--55\%) on the new ones, against 20\% and 27\% for the best rule with default settings; three replications without stored responses give 42--43\%. Much of the gain comes from not acting on alarms shared by many stations: a rule that vetoes such alarms recovers 34\% and 40\%, and the LLM's margin over it is 8--9 points. How much the veto is worth depends on what a false repair costs. With long repairs, a stopped line, full congestion or mild faults, the LLM hybrid loses far less than any rule; with severe faults, short repairs or no congestion, all planners are level; and when one cause degrades four stations at once, the veto discards real faults and the hybrid falls behind the plain rule. A first evaluation design, with the fault injected at a fixed time inside the detector's start-up burst, had produced apparent gains that disappeared once the design was corrected.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Our earlier autonomic architecture detected conditions but never planned or acted.
\item Its alarms are sparse line-wide bursts; with them no planner recovers delay.
\item A per-station change test on processing times can make closed-loop repair pay off.
\item A frontier LLM vetoing the test's candidates leads where false repairs are costly.
\item A burst-veto rule gets most of that gain; with common-cause faults both lose.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomic computing proposes systems that manage themselves through a Monitor--Analyze--Plan--Execute loop over a shared Knowledge base (MAPE-K)~\cite{kephart2003vision,weyns2020introduction}. In manufacturing, this loop is attractive because production lines degrade continuously and human supervision does not scale: a self-healing line would notice a degrading station and reconfigure itself before the delay accumulates.

In earlier work we proposed an architecture for self-coordinated manufacturing processes with self-* properties and evaluated it with a simulator, SSACSimulator, over the Bosch production-line dataset~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar,sanchez2020jii}. Revisiting that code, we found that it implements the Monitor and Analyze phases only. When one of its three detectors fires, the simulator writes \emph{``launching the autonomous cycle for reconfiguration''} to a log panel; no plan is produced and nothing is executed. The loop was announced, not closed.

This paper closes it, and asks what closing it actually requires. Two developments make the question timely. First, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed as planners for industrial decision making, from the control of modular production systems~\cite{xia2023towards} to advanced planning and scheduling~\cite{li2026a4ps}. Second, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020rag} offers a natural candidate for the Knowledge component of MAPE-K: instead of a hand-built ontology, the planner retrieves the operational history it needs. Whether either helps, against which baseline, and under which Analyze layer, is an empirical question.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A faithful, tested, headless port of the original simulator~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar}, and an analysis of its Analyze layer on the published data: one detector is unreachable, one is exercised by 0.004\% of the events, and the third raises line-wide bursts of alarms, concentrated at the start of each trace, that name a faulty station within a day in only half of the scenarios (Sections~\ref{sec:revisit} and~\ref{sec:legacy}).
  \item The missing Plan, Execute and Knowledge phases, and a per-station Analyze on processing times: an action space based on the parallel ``twin'' stations of the Bosch line, a symbolic validator, fault injection with three fault shapes, and an explicit, parameterised effect model (Section~\ref{sec:loop}).
  \item A comparison of rule-based planners against LLM planners with and without retrieved knowledge, and of a hybrid in which a statistical test proposes and the LLM vetoes, on a local 7B model and a frontier model, under both Analyze layers, with parameters and prompts fixed on a development trace after a redesign that the held-out traces informed (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), 216 fault scenarios on three held-out traces and 360 on five traces generated after the design was frozen (Sections~\ref{sec:planners}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Evidence that the Analyze layer sets what any planner can recover and the planner how much of it is recovered; that, given useful alarms, a capable LLM adds most as a veto over a statistical test, by avoiding repairs that cost more than they fix; that a plain rule vetoing alarm bursts reproduces most of that gain; that the veto is worth much where false repairs are costly, little where they are cheap, and harmful under common-cause faults; and that a fixed fault onset can manufacture conclusions that disappear under a sounder design (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

All code, data-processing scripts, prompts, every model decision with its stated reason, and all results are available (see ``Data and code availability'' at the end of the paper).

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Autonomic computing and self-adaptive systems}
The MAPE-K reference model~\cite{kephart2003vision} structures a self-managing system as a feedback loop around a managed resource. Engineering such loops is the subject of the self-adaptive systems literature~\cite{weyns2020introduction}, which stresses that the Analyze phase must separate genuine change from noise before any adaptation is planned. LLMs have recently been examined as components of such loops: a state-of-the-art review and research roadmap organises their potential uses around the MAPE-K functions~\cite{li2024genai,li2024exploring}; LLM-based agents have been embedded in MAPE-K-based multi-agent systems~\cite{nascimento2023selfadaptive}; and MAPER extends MAPE-K with an LLM-based reasoning component to handle unanticipated runtime situations~\cite{alves2026maper}. Our study differs in placing an LLM inside the loop of an industrial system whose detector we first inherit unchanged and then replace, and in measuring it against tuned non-LLM baselines on held-out data and on data generated after the design was frozen.

\paragraph{Self-* properties in manufacturing}
Our earlier work integrated multi-agent systems, service orientation and autonomic computing for Industry~4.0 process coordination~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar,sanchez2020jii,sanchez2020survey}. The present paper re-examines that architecture empirically rather than proposing a new one.

\paragraph{Alarm management and change detection}
In process industries, alarm management standards require alarms to be rationalised, so that each one is meaningful and calls for a response, before operators are asked to act on them~\cite{isa182}; a technical report applies the same principles to batch and discrete manufacturing~\cite{isatr1826}, the setting of a line such as Bosch's. An autonomic controller that acts on alarms faces the same requirement, without an operator to absorb the noise. Detecting a change in the rate of an event stream or in the distribution of a measurement is a classical problem~\cite{basseville1993detection}; our rule-based planners and our per-station detector are simple instances of it.

\paragraph{LLMs and retrieval in industrial decision making}
RAG conditions a language model on documents retrieved for each query~\cite{lewis2020rag}. In manufacturing, LLM agents have been combined with digital twins to plan and control modular production~\cite{xia2023towards} and applied to advanced planning and scheduling~\cite{li2026a4ps}, and a recent review of LLMs for fault diagnosis in smart manufacturing covers, among other roles, retrieval-augmented fusion of industrial knowledge~\cite{thuan2026review}, and another reviews how humans are kept in the loop when LLMs are used in smart manufacturing~\cite{sadeqi2026hitl}. Here, the retrieved knowledge is structured operational statistics computed by code from the controller's own history, not documents; we keep the term RAG for the retrieval step and state exactly what is retrieved (Section~\ref{sec:planners}).

\section{The original system revisited}
\label{sec:revisit}

\subsection{Simulator and data}
SSACSimulator is a Qt/C++ discrete-event simulator. A process model, derived from the Bosch Production Line Performance dataset~\cite{bosch2016kaggle}, describes 52 stations on four sub-lines plus begin and end nodes, 285 transitions (84 of them main flows), a throughput time of 6\,420~min and 1\,183\,165 cases. Four event traces of 100\,000 transitions each, prepared for the original study~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar}, replay recorded piece movements; they span 54 to 168 hours of production. For validation we cut five more traces from the same raw files, after the design was frozen (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). All traces come from the dataset's test set: its 1\,183\,748 pieces, less 583 without any timestamp, give the 1\,183\,165 cases of the process model. The events of all these pieces are sorted by time and cut into consecutive chunks of 100\,000, so each trace is a contiguous period of production that contains every test-set piece active in it, but not the pieces of the dataset's training set, which the original study did not use. Timestamps are anonymised; as in the original study, one unit of the dataset is taken as 600~min, so that its resolution of 0.01 units gives the 6-min granularity of the traces. Production is recorded in batches: 62 of the 157 hours of trace 100m--200m contain no event.

\subsection{Headless port}
We ported the simulation core (about 1\,440 lines of C++ logic, excluding the graphical user interface) to Python. The port reproduces the original's behaviour as read from its source, including details that change results: thresholds computed in single precision, a 100\,000-event loading limit, and a lenient rule for malformed rows. A fast path advances only the pieces that can leave the system; the original per-tick loop is kept as a test oracle, and a regression test checks that both produce identical detections, decisions and piece lifetimes. Parity is with the original as read from its source, not with the compiled Qt binary. A run takes 0.2--0.4~s.

\subsection{What the Analyze layer actually does}
The original implements three detectors: a station whose mean transition time exceeds 1.3 times its reference; more than 10\% of stations flagged within one batch of simultaneous events; and a mean piece lifetime exceeding 1.1 times the throughput time. Only the second fires. The predicted-failure detector depends on a classifier label that is positive in 15 of 400\,000 events across all traces (0.004\%). The global detector is unreachable: the mean piece lifetime (636~min) is an order of magnitude below its threshold (7\,062~min). Finally, 45 of the 285 transitions have a reference time of zero, so any positive duration flags their station. Section~\ref{sec:legacy} shows what the one active detector sees once faults are injected.

Plan, Execute and Knowledge are absent. This is the starting point for the rest of the paper.

\subsection{Twin stations and the action space}
The Bosch line is built from parallel station pairs (and a few triples) that share predecessors and successors and split the load almost evenly; for example, on their main flows stations L0\_S2 and L0\_S3 receive 339\,345 and 334\,054 cases. We identify twins automatically from the overlap of their predecessor and successor sets on main flows: 29 of the 52 stations have one. Diverting a piece from a station to its twin keeps its trajectory consistent with the process graph. Structural equivalence does not guarantee equivalent equipment or capacity, which the anonymised dataset does not describe; a plant would have to confirm each twin. The action space is \textsc{Reroute}(S, T, $f$), which diverts a fraction $f$ of the pieces entering S to its twin T; \textsc{Restore}(S); \textsc{Maintenance}(S, $d$), which repairs S for $d$ minutes; and \textsc{NoOp}.

\subsection{Symbolic validator}
Every action, from any planner, is checked against the process graph and the current interventions before execution: stations must exist, a reroute target must be a structural twin, reroutes must not form chains, and a station cannot be repaired twice concurrently. The validator never judges whether an action is a good idea, only whether it is admissible.

\subsection{Effect model}
The simulation is trace-driven: start times come from the recorded data and are not shifted; faults and actions act on transition durations, and the lifetime of a piece is the sum of its transition durations. Delay therefore does not propagate as queueing; the model estimates time lost per piece. A Bosch ``transition'' is the time between two station timestamps and mostly measures waiting: its median is 0~min and its 90th percentile 18--30~min, but its 99th percentile reaches 4\,100--5\,900~min. Effects are therefore \emph{additive} and proportional to each station's processing time $\tau_S$ (the median of its transitions of up to 60~min), never multiplicative over a transition that includes days of storage. $\tau_S$ is 6 or 12~min for the eight stations where faults are injected, identical on all traces. The effect model is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fault} of factor $k$ on S: $+(k(t)-1)\,\tau_S$ on every transition leaving S, where $k(t)$ is a \emph{step} ($k$ from $t_0$), \emph{intermittent} ($k$ for 60~min, 1 for 120~min, repeating) or \emph{drift} (rising linearly from 1 to $k$ over 600~min).
  \item \textbf{Reroute}: a deterministic fraction of pieces (selected by a hash of the case identifier) goes to T, which then adds $\alpha\,f\,\tau_T$ for the extra load.
  \item \textbf{Maintenance}: faults on S already active when the repair starts are cleared when it ends; a fault that begins during a repair is not. An intermittent fault counts as active from its onset, also during its off phases. During the repair, in \emph{contingency} mode (default) backup capacity outside the model processes the pieces, adding $0.5\,\tau_S$; in \emph{stop} mode the pieces wait for the repair to finish. Repairing a healthy station costs and fixes nothing, which is what makes false alarms expensive.
\end{itemize}
Because the model is additive, the excess delay of a run decomposes exactly into the minutes added to individual transitions, which lets us measure it within any time window. The dataset is anonymised and provides no capacities, repair times or labelled failures, so all faults are synthetic and every parameter we could not ground in data (severity $k$, repair time $d$, congestion $\alpha$, maintenance mode) is varied in a sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:sweep}).

\subsection{A per-station Analyze on processing times}
\label{sec:perstation}
Section~\ref{sec:legacy} shows that the legacy detector reports the injected faults rarely and only inside line-wide bursts. Its station test compares means of whole transitions, dominated by hours or days of storage, so a fault that adds minutes of processing barely moves them. We therefore add an alternative Analyze layer that looks only at the processing regime: the transitions leaving a station that last at most 60~min. For each station, causally, it takes as reference the 75th percentile of those transitions over the previous 24~h and the rate $p_0$ at which they exceed it (Laplace-smoothed, because timestamps have a 6-min granularity); among the station's last $N$ processing transitions, it counts those above the reference and flags the station when that count is improbable under Binomial$(N, p_0)$ at level $\alpha$. Nothing is flagged during an 8-h warm-up; the stations flagged in one batch of simultaneous events are reported in a single alarm, and a station is not flagged again within a refractory period. $N$, $\alpha$ and the refractory period were chosen on the development trace (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}): $N=10$, $\alpha=10^{-3}$ and 6~min. With this layer, the legacy station alarms are no longer passed to the planners. The planners themselves are unchanged.

\section{Planners}
\label{sec:planners}

All planners receive, for each alarm, the alarm and the state of their own past interventions. None sees the injected faults. All of them respond to a station judged faulty in the same way: reroute to a free twin if one exists, then repair. Every planner, including the LLM planners, leaves a station it has acted on alone for 240~min.

\subsection{Rule-based planners}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Naive}: acts on every station named in an alarm.
  \item \textbf{Calibrated}: acts when the number of alarms on a station within a 120-min window is improbable (Poisson tail, $\alpha=10^{-3}$, at least 3 alarms) under its base rate, learned on a \emph{different} fault-free trace.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive}: the same test, against the rate observed in the preceding 24~h of the same trace, causally, after an 8~h warm-up.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive + load veto} (control): the adaptive rule, ignoring stations that receive load the controller itself rerouted onto them.
  \item \textbf{Twin-only} (control): acts on every flagged station that has a free twin and ignores the others.
  \item \textbf{Burst veto} (control): the adaptive rule, rejecting the candidates of an alarm that names at least $m$ stations, or of any alarm while at least $m$ distinct stations have alarmed in the last $W$~min; with or without the load veto. As in the hybrid, a rejected station is not re-examined for 120~min. $m$ and $W$ were chosen on the development trace (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}): with the load veto, alarms of at least 3 stations; without it, at least 8 stations alarming within 120~min.
  \item \textbf{Random veto} (control): the adaptive rule, rejecting each candidate at random with the hybrid's per-candidate veto rate on the held-out traces (70\%), with the same 120-min suspension; five seeds.
\end{itemize}
The controls are not proposed methods; they implement as plain rules behaviours observed in LLM planners, so that an LLM's contribution is measured beyond them. The burst veto is the reason the hybrid on Fable~5.1 gives most often for its vetoes (Section~\ref{sec:vetoes}). The default window, level and history above were fixed before any evaluation; we also re-tune them on the development trace (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{LLM planners}
For every alarm with actionable stations, the LLM must return, for each of them, a verdict (\emph{degraded}, \emph{normal}, \emph{self-inflicted} or \emph{insufficient evidence}) followed by an action type (none, repair, or reroute and repair). The output is constrained by a JSON schema whose station names are restricted to the actionable ones, so the decoder cannot invent a station. Code chooses the twin, discards any action inconsistent with the model's own verdict, and passes the rest to the validator.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1} receives the alarm, the actionable stations, whether each has a free twin, and the current interventions: the same structural information as the rules.
  \item \textbf{C2 (RAG)} additionally receives knowledge retrieved from the controller's operational memory: for each station, the alarms observed in the last 2 and 24~h against the number its fault-free base rate predicts, whether the controller is pushing rerouted load onto it, whether its alarms dropped after past repairs, and its most recent log records. Retrieval is keyed by station and ordered by recency; all statistics are computed by code.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid}: the adaptive change test proposes the stations; the LLM, with C2's knowledge, confirms or vetoes and chooses the action. A vetoed station is not re-examined for 120~min. The prompt tells the model that the test is usually right but cannot see context, and gives as examples load the controller rerouted onto a station, a past repair that did not reduce its alarms, and \emph{``a burst of alarms shared by many stations at once''}.
\end{itemize}
The system prompt describes how the Analyze layer raises alarms. Prompt version v2 describes the legacy detector and was fixed on the development trace before any held-out run; v3, used with the per-station detector, differs from v2 only in that paragraph, and was written after the held-out runs with the legacy detector and tested on the development trace with the 7B model only (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Table~\ref{tab:ablation} measures what that paragraph and the burst example change.

\paragraph{Models}
We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2024}, 4-bit quantised, served locally with Ollama~\cite{ollama} at temperature~0; and Claude Fable~5.1 (Anthropic), accessed through the Claude Agent SDK~\cite{anthropic2026agentsdk} with effort set to \emph{low} (SDK version 0.2.163). Each call replaces the harness's system prompt with the planner's, loads no settings or memory files (\texttt{setting\_sources} empty, an empty temporary working directory), exposes no tools and no MCP servers, and enforces the planner's JSON schema as structured output. The stored usage is consistent with this: a call takes a median of 2\,431 input tokens, for 4\,042 characters of system prompt, request and schema. Every response comes from Fable~5.1: in 19\,187 of the 19\,189 stored calls, the output tokens the SDK attributes to Fable~5.1 equal those of the response; the harness's own short internal calls (a median of 16 output tokens) produce no part of it. The frontier model does not expose a temperature setting, so its runs are not bit-reproducible. Every request and response of both models is stored, keyed by the full request. Identical requests are frequent, because before the fault onset a scenario yields the same states as the fault-free run of its trace, and they are served from this store: with the per-station detector, 2\,403 of the 26\,933 calls of C2 on Fable~5.1 were new. A new call takes a median of 5--8~s with either model, short against faults that cost hours. Each decision is a single sample of the model; to measure run-to-run variability we repeated the main hybrid run three times, each with its own key in the response store (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Fable~5.1 was used through a Claude subscription, with model identifier \texttt{claude-fable-5-1}, between 6 and 17 October 2026. A first development pilot was discarded because the SDK passed the tool definitions of the account's connectors to the model; the harness was then restricted to no tools and its identifier added to the cache key, so that no response from that pilot was reused. The prompts are given in \ref{app:prompts}.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Scenarios}
A fault of factor $k=3$ is injected on one of eight stations, four with a twin (L0\_S2, L0\_S4, L0\_S6, L3\_S35) and four without (L0\_S1, L0\_S8, L3\_S29, L3\_S33), all of which have observable processing times on every trace, with one of the three fault shapes, at one of three onsets per station and shape. Onsets are drawn uniformly between 24~h after the first event of the trace and 24~h before its last, from a generator seeded by the scenario's name. Each run stops 24~h after the onset. Each trace also has a fault-free run over its whole length. Repairs last 60~min, $\alpha=0.5$, and maintenance runs in contingency mode unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{Development and held-out traces}
The first trace (0--100m) is the development trace; the other three are held out (216 fault scenarios). On the development trace only, we chose the parameters of the per-station detector (27 configurations of $N \in \{10, 20, 40\}$, $\alpha \in \{10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}\}$ and refractory periods of 6, 30 and 60~min, selected by the recovery of the adaptive rule with load veto, so that the detector was tuned for a rule, not for the LLM planners), re-tuned the rules (45 configurations of window, level and history for each adaptive rule, 15 of window and level for the calibrated rule), and fixed the prompts. Every prompt version tried and its results are logged in the repository. Calibrated rules and C2 learn their base rates on a fault-free trace other than the one they are evaluated on (100m--200m from the development trace; 200m--300m and 500m--600m from each other). During prompt development, the development runs took their base rates from 100m--200m, which is later held out; this exposes no fault labels.

\paragraph{Sequence of decisions}
The held-out traces were not untouched by the redesign, and we state how. The evaluation design (onsets, repair rule, horizon) was revised after a first evaluation on the held-out traces (Section~\ref{sec:designs}). Under the revised design, the rules with the legacy detector recovered at most 4\% on the held-out traces and the development trace had no legacy alarm after its first 24~h, so every rule configuration scored zero there; this is what led us to build the per-station detector. Before any run with it, we registered its form in the repository: the processing regime, the 24-h reference, the binomial test, the warm-up and a refractory period of 60~min, with $N$ and $\alpha$ to be chosen on the development trace. We then added the refractory period to the grid, because with 60~min a station can never reach the three alarms in 120~min that the rules require; 6~min was chosen. Prompt v3 was written then, and tested on the development trace with the 7B model only. All held-out results with the per-station detector were obtained with these choices fixed. C1 on Fable~5.1 was run after the hybrid's held-out results were known. The mild-fault condition and the ablations of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} were registered before they were run, after the main held-out results. So were, later, the veto controls, the remaining sensitivity conditions, the common-cause faults, the replications and C2 on the validation traces; the controls' parameters were chosen on the development trace. A note on the refractory period, added after the fact, is in the same log.

\paragraph{Validation traces}
To test the frozen design on data that informed no choice, we generated five further traces from the raw dataset~\cite{bosch2016kaggle} with a Python~3 port of the original preprocessing scripts, which reproduces the four existing traces row for row. We took, in order, the first five unused chunks of 100\,000 events (300m--400m, 400m--500m, 600m--700m, 700m--800m and 800m--900m; 104 to 177~h each), with exclusion rules registered before generating them; none was excluded. Everything else was frozen: design, stations, shapes, three onsets per station and shape (360 fault scenarios), detector, prompts, models and response store. Base rates come from the development trace. The planners are the rules with default and re-tuned settings, the veto controls, the two hybrids and C2 on Fable~5.1; C1 was not run there.

\paragraph{Metrics}
Every run replays the same trace, so each piece is compared with itself in a fault-free reference run. The \emph{24-h recovery} of a planner over a set of scenarios is $1-\sum E_{24}(\text{planner}) / \sum E_{24}(\text{no action})$, where $E_{24}$ is the delay added to transitions in the 24~h after the fault onset: 1 erases the cost of the faults; 0 is no better than doing nothing; negative values mean that the planner's own actions cost more than the faults. The recovery is therefore net of the cost of acting. Because the effect model is additive, $E_{24}$ splits exactly into the fault's own delay that remains (on transitions not covered by a repair or a reroute) and the delay caused by the planner's actions; we report this split on the scenarios in which the fault costs something within the window (Table~\ref{tab:split}). The window is chronological: production is recorded in batches, so a station may have few or no transitions in it. A planner \emph{acts} on a scenario if it repairs the faulty station within those 24~h; \emph{false repairs} are repairs of other stations in the same window. The \emph{false-alarm cost} is the excess delay per hour in the fault-free runs.

\paragraph{Statistics}
The primary estimand is the difference in 24-h recovery between two planners, with a 95\% bootstrap interval~\cite{efron1993bootstrap} that resamples scenarios within their trace (10\,000 resamples). Because scenarios on the same trace share their alarm noise and three traces are few clusters, we also report a two-level bootstrap that resamples traces and then scenarios, a bootstrap that resamples the 24 (held-out) or 40 (validation) clusters of trace and faulty station, because the 24-h windows of scenarios on the same trace overlap and share decisions, the difference with each trace left out, and the difference in every ordered draw of the traces with replacement (27 for three traces, 3\,125 for five). Intervals for a single planner's recovery use 4\,000 resamples. These are checks of stability over the traces we have, not estimates of how the result would generalise to other periods or lines. We report the number of scenarios in which one planner loses less time than the other and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r$ as effect size; the Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-scenario delay~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, with Holm adjustment~\cite{holm1979}, treat scenarios as independent and are given in the repository only. The breakdown by fault shape is exploratory.

\subsection{The legacy Analyze layer gives too little evidence}

\subsection{A per-station Analyze layer}
\label{sec:sweep}
The per-station detector does not report the faulty station more often relative to its background than the legacy detector does: the ratio of alarms with and without the fault is 4.1 against 4.5, and it names a station that has no fault in half of the windows (Table~\ref{tab:detection}). What it changes is when and how. It reports the faulty station a median of 3.5~h after the onset instead of 10.9~h, and in alarms that list 2.3 stations instead of 6.5 (Section~\ref{sec:legacy}), so that a rate test on its alarms can single the station out. The binomial test is what keeps it usable: the threshold control, with the same processing regime and windows, detects slightly more faults but raises 4.4 alarms per hour without faults instead of 1.6, and with it the rules fall to between $-0.39$ and $-0.35$. The binomial level is not a false-alarm rate: consecutive transitions of a station are correlated by batch and product, so the nominal $\alpha=10^{-3}$ per test still yields 1.6 alarms per hour on a fault-free line. On the development trace, every configuration of the per-station detector names the faulty station within 24~h in all 72 development scenarios; the chosen one lets the adaptive rule with load veto recover $+0.84$ there.

\subsection{Statistical comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:stats} compares the hybrid on Fable~5.1 with the other planners under the per-station detector. Its closest competitor is the burst-veto control with load veto: the hybrid recovers $+0.08$ more $[+0.01, +0.14]$, loses less production time in 124 scenarios and more in 19, and is ahead on each held-out trace ($+0.07$, $+0.12$ and $+0.05$) and in all 27 ordered draws of three traces; by shape, the margin is clear only on drift faults. Against the re-tuned calibrated rule the hybrid gains on intermittent ($+0.25$ $[+0.12, +0.37]$) and drift faults ($+0.22$ $[+0.14, +0.32]$), and the two are level on step faults ($+0.03$ $[-0.08, +0.15]$). \paragraph{Timing, losses and calls}
Over a shorter window of 8~h after the onset, the ranking is the same: the hybrid on Fable~5.1 recovers $+0.36$, the hybrid on the 7B model $+0.20$, the adaptive rule with load veto $+0.11$ and C2 on Fable~5.1 $+0.09$. The hybrid does not act sooner: when it repairs the faulty station, its median first action comes 345~min after the onset, against 311~min for the rule and 218~min for the calibrated rule. Its advantage is not paid for with large losses against doing nothing: in its worst scenario it adds 2\,031 piece-min to the delay of doing nothing (the mean cost of doing nothing is 4\,881 piece-min per scenario), against 7\,902 for the adaptive rule with load veto. Its largest losses against the rule come from vetoing real faults: in the two worst scenarios, step faults on 200m--300m, the rule removed about 60\% of the fault and the hybrid nothing, a difference of 13\,941 and 14\,508 piece-min. The hybrid calls the model about 20 times per scenario (median 22) and 0.2--0.3 times per hour without faults; C2 calls it 1.0--1.5 times per hour. Charging a fixed cost for every repair, as a maintenance budget would, only widens the gap: at 240 piece-min per repair the hybrid still recovers $+0.31$ on the held-out traces and $+0.35$ on the validation traces, while the adaptive rule with load veto falls to $-0.09$ and $-0.19$ and the re-tuned calibrated rule recovers $+0.17$ on the validation traces.

Common-cause faults turn the veto against itself. When one fault degrades four stations at once, their alarms are, by construction, shared bursts; the burst control vetoes them and falls to $+0.36$, and the hybrid on Fable~5.1 to $+0.43$, $0.10$ below the adaptive rule with load veto ($[-0.17, -0.05]$). \subsection{Why the evaluation design matters}
\label{sec:designs}
In a first phase of this research, every fault was injected 600~min after the first event of a trace, a repair cleared any fault that began during it, and recovery was measured over the whole trace. Under that design the legacy detector appeared to work: the adaptive rules recovered 52--54\%, C2 on Fable~5.1 58\% and the hybrid on Fable~5.1 24\% (Table~\ref{tab:designs}). Those gains were an artefact of the design. The fixed onset fell inside the detector's start-up burst of alarms and right after the 8-h warm-up of the planners' memory, when C2 repairs many stations at once; routine repairs then coincided with the fault or preceded it by minutes and, under the old repair rule, cleared it at no cost. Moving onsets out of the start-up period, letting repairs clear only active faults and bounding the metric to 24~h removed those gains; the ranking of the two LLM designs reversed only with the per-station detector (under the legacy detector, C2 on Fable~5.1 stays ahead of the hybrid, $+0.07$ against $+0.03$). We report the first design because the error is easy to make: a closed-loop controller evaluated at a single, fixed fault time inherits whatever happens to the line at that time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The Analyze layer and the planner interact}
Closing the original loop~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar} on its own detector recovers almost nothing within a day: no planner recovers more than 0.07, because the detector reports a fault late, rarely and inside line-wide bursts. Replacing it with a per-station test on processing times moves the best rule from $-0.02$ to $+0.20$ and the best planner from $+0.07$ to $+0.41$. But the planner matters as much once the alarms are useful: under the per-station detector the planners span 0.66, from $-0.25$ to $+0.41$. The Analyze layer sets what a planner can achieve; the planner decides how much of it is achieved.

\paragraph{Propose and veto: the value is in not acting}
Given useful alarms, the LLM adds most as a veto over a statistical test, and it does so by declining to act, not by detecting more. The split of the delay shows the effect: the rule removes more of the fault, but its false repairs cost more than the extra fault it removes; the hybrid leaves more of the fault and pays a third of the cost. Most of this is a simple argument that a rule can implement: do not repair a station whose alarm is shared by many others. A rule that does only that, with the load veto, reproduces two thirds to three quarters of the hybrid's margin over the adaptive rule (from $+0.20$ to $+0.34$ on the held-out traces, from $+0.17$ to $+0.40$ on the validation traces). What the LLM adds beyond it, $+0.08$ and $+0.09$, is consistent with applying the argument selectively: in the stored decisions it vetoes the faulty station less often than a healthy one in alarms of every size (Table~\ref{tab:vetosize}), although the counts for the faulty station are small and not independent. That selectivity may come from a statistic the model is given rather than from judgement only a model can supply: the retrieved knowledge includes each station's alarms over 24~h against its base rate, and no control rule uses it. Declining alone is not enough: vetoing at random does not help, and C1 on the frontier model, without the test or retrieved knowledge, barely acts and recovers little. On their own, both LLM planners do worse than the rule.

\paragraph{The veto is worth what a false repair costs}
The sensitivity analysis gives the result its scope (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). Where false repairs are expensive (mild faults, long repairs, a stopped line, full congestion), the hybrid on Fable~5.1 is by far the best planner, often the only one that does not do much more harm than good. Where acting is cheap (severe faults, short repairs, no congestion), every reasonable planner recovers about half of the delay and the LLM adds nothing. And when faults really do have a common cause, the burst argument discards them: the hybrid falls behind the plain rule, and the 7B hybrid, which does not use the argument, does best. The value of an LLM veto is therefore not a property of the model but of the plant: it depends on the cost of an unnecessary intervention and on whether alarms shared by several stations are, there, usually noise or usually a common fault.

\paragraph{The model matters inside the right architecture}
The same hybrid recovers $+0.41$ on Fable~5.1 and $+0.29$ on the 7B model on the held-out traces, and $+0.49$ against $+0.23$ on the new traces, where the 7B hybrid falls below the best rules. Only the frontier model's advantage replicates. This is a contrast between two systems, which differ in size, quantisation, provider and inference settings, not a measured capability curve.

\paragraph{Implications for practice}
Within the limits of a study with synthetic faults, four points transfer to automation architects and maintenance engineers. First, connecting threshold alarms directly to maintenance orders is harmful: the naive planner loses against doing nothing under both detectors, because false repairs accumulate; alarms must be rationalised before they drive actions, by analogy with what alarm-management practice requires for operators~\cite{isa182,isatr1826}. Second, a detector should compare each station with its own recent behaviour on the part of the signal a fault affects, here processing times rather than storage-dominated transitions, and with a test that controls its false alarms. Third, whether acting pays off depends on the severity of faults relative to the cost of a repair and on backup capacity during repairs; before adding an LLM, a plant should try a rule that suppresses actions on alarms shared by many stations, which here recovers most of what the LLM does, and should check how often its shared alarms are in fact common-cause faults. Fourth, an LLM veto is worth its cost where unnecessary interventions are expensive; its non-determinism, its sensitivity to how alarms are structured and its failure under common-cause faults argue for keeping every action behind a symbolic validator, as here, and, in a plant, behind human approval in the maintenance management system that issues the work orders.

\paragraph{Development sets mislead}
The per-station detector and the re-tuned rules were chosen on the development trace, where the detector names the faulty station in all 72 scenarios and the adaptive rule recovers $+0.84$; on the held-out traces the detector names it in 122 of 216 and the same rule recovers $+0.20$, and the configurations re-tuned there help one rule and not the others. A single trace is too small to select designs on, and LLM-based controllers should be evaluated on data not used to write their prompts, with fault onsets that do not coincide with the controller's start-up.

\paragraph{Threats to validity}
\emph{Construct}: faults are synthetic, and the effect model is additive and trace-driven, without queueing, so every recovery is a property of the model, not a measured property of the Bosch line; its parameters were varied one at a time for all planners (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}), but not jointly, and the frozen prompt describes 60-min repairs with backup capacity in every condition. Common-cause faults were tested in one form only (one fault on four stations at once, 54 scenarios). Recovery is net of the cost of acting, measured in production time within 24~h of the fault; a fixed cost per repair, which we tried as a check, only widens the hybrid's lead, but we use no economic weights. A 24-h window is chronological, not productive time, and in about a third of the scenarios the fault costs nothing in it (Table~\ref{tab:split}). The per-station detector's reference is the station's previous 24~h, so a persistent fault is absorbed into it after about a day. Twins are structural, not verified equipment equivalents. \emph{Internal}: the held-out traces informed the decision to build a new detector and the evaluation design, though not their parameters (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); the validation traces were generated after everything was frozen. Each frontier-model decision is a stored sample at low effort, and most calls reuse a stored response, which also makes scenarios share decisions; three replications without the stored responses changed the main recovery by at most 0.014. Prompt v3 was tested on the development trace with the 7B model only; the prompts are part of the treatment; the control rules cover the load veto and the burst argument but not every statistic C2 retrieves, such as the outcome of past repairs; the 7B model is 4-bit quantised. The frontier model may have seen our earlier papers~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar,sanchez2020jii}, which name the Bosch line, but the faults are synthetic and not described anywhere it could have seen. \emph{External}: one production line, one dataset, the test-set pieces only; two language models, with no intermediate sizes. \emph{Statistical}: the 24-h windows of scenarios on the same trace overlap; the held-out set has three traces and the validation set five, and resampling traces or clusters of trace and station addresses the dependence only partly.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We closed the autonomic loop of a self-coordinated manufacturing architecture that, as published, only detected. On its own detector, no planner recovers delay: the detector raises line-wide bursts of alarms and reports a faulty station late and rarely. With a per-station change test on processing times, and within our effect model, a frontier LLM with retrieved operational statistics that vetoes the test's candidates recovers about two fifths of the delay within a day on the held-out traces and half on five traces generated after the design was frozen, ahead of every rule we tried, and three replications reproduce it. But a plain rule that vetoes alarms shared by many stations gets most of the way there, and the LLM's remaining margin, 8--9 points, is consistent with applying that argument selectively, a pattern a rule using the retrieved alarm statistics might also capture. The veto is worth much where false repairs are expensive, nothing where acting is cheap, and it hurts when faults have a common cause. Used on its own, the same model does not beat the test, and the 7B model's advantage does not replicate. An earlier evaluation, with a fixed fault time, had suggested the opposite conclusions. Future work will validate the effect model against plant data with real capacities, repair times and common-cause faults, add intermediate model sizes, and test the detector on faults that persist beyond a day.
