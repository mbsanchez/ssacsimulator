\title{From detection to action: closing the autonomic loop of a self-coordinated manufacturing line with rule-based and retrieval-augmented LLM planners}

\author[a,b]{Manuel S\'anchez\corref{cor1}}
\ead{mbsanchez@unet.edu.ve}
\author[b]{Ernesto Exposito}
\ead{ernesto.exposito@univ-pau.fr}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[a]{Universidad Nacional Experimental del T\'achira, San Crist\'obal, Venezuela}
\address[b]{Univ Pau \& Pays Adour / E2S UPPA, Laboratoire d'informatique de l'Universit\'e de Pau et des Pays de l'Adour (LIUPPA), EA3000, 64000 Pau, France}

\begin{abstract}
Autonomic architectures for Industry~4.0 promise self-healing production, but published systems often stop at detection. We revisit our own earlier self-coordinated manufacturing architecture, find that it implements Monitor and Analyze but not Plan, Execute or a Knowledge base, port its simulator to a tested headless engine over the Bosch production-line dataset, and close the loop with an action space built on the line's parallel twin stations, a symbolic validator, synthetic step, intermittent and drift faults, and an explicit effect model. We compare rule-based planners with large-language-model (LLM) planners, with and without retrieved operational knowledge, on a local 7B model (Qwen2.5) and a frontier model (Claude Fable~5.1), selecting every design choice on a development trace and evaluating on 216 fault scenarios from three held-out traces, by the delay recovered within 24~h of the fault. The original Analyze layer is the bottleneck: within a day of a fault it names the faulty station in only half of the scenarios, a few times and always inside line-wide bursts of alarms, and with it no planner recovers more than 7\% of the delay. A per-station detector on processing times, chosen on the development trace, lets the best rule recover 20\%. With that detector, a frontier LLM that vetoes the candidates of a statistical change test recovers 41\% (95\% interval 32--50\%), ahead of every rule configuration we tried, including one chosen on the held-out traces, and of the same design on the 7B model (29\%); used without the test, the same model does not beat the rule. A first evaluation design, with the fault injected at a fixed time inside the detector's start-up burst, had produced apparent gains that reversed this ranking.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}
\item Our earlier autonomic architecture detected conditions but never planned or acted.
\item Its alarms are sparse line-wide bursts; with them no planner recovers delay.
\item A per-station detector on processing times makes closed-loop repair worthwhile.
\item A frontier LLM vetoing a statistical test's candidates doubles the best rule.
\item A fixed fault onset inside the alarm burst had produced misleading gains.
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Autonomic computing proposes systems that manage themselves through a Monitor--Analyze--Plan--Execute loop over a shared Knowledge base (MAPE-K)~\cite{kephart2003vision,weyns2020introduction}. In manufacturing, this loop is attractive because production lines degrade continuously and human supervision does not scale: a self-healing line would notice a degrading station and reconfigure itself before the delay accumulates.

In earlier work we proposed an architecture for self-coordinated manufacturing processes with self-* properties and evaluated it with a simulator, SSACSimulator, over the Bosch production-line dataset~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar,sanchez2020jii}. Revisiting that code, we found that it implements the Monitor and Analyze phases only. When one of its three detectors fires, the simulator writes \emph{``launching the autonomous cycle for reconfiguration''} to a log panel; no plan is produced and nothing is executed. The loop was announced, not closed.

This paper closes it, and asks what closing it actually requires. Two developments make the question timely. First, large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed as planners for industrial decision making, from the control of modular production systems~\cite{xia2023towards} to advanced planning and scheduling~\cite{li2026a4ps}. Second, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)~\cite{lewis2020rag} offers a natural candidate for the Knowledge component of MAPE-K: instead of a hand-built ontology, the planner retrieves the operational history it needs. Whether either helps, against which baseline, and under which Analyze layer, is an empirical question.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A faithful, tested, headless port of the original simulator~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar}, and an analysis of its Analyze layer on the published data: one detector is unreachable, one is exercised by 0.004\% of the events, and the third raises line-wide bursts of alarms, concentrated at the start of each trace, that name a faulty station within a day in only half of the scenarios (Sections~\ref{sec:revisit} and~\ref{sec:legacy}).
  \item The missing Plan, Execute and Knowledge phases, and a per-station Analyze on processing times: an action space based on the parallel ``twin'' stations of the Bosch line, a symbolic validator, fault injection with three fault shapes, and an explicit, parameterised effect model (Section~\ref{sec:loop}).
  \item A comparison of rule-based planners against LLM planners with and without retrieved knowledge, and of a hybrid in which a statistical test proposes and the LLM vetoes, on a local 7B model and a frontier model, under both Analyze layers, with every design choice made on a development trace and 216 fault scenarios on three held-out traces (Sections~\ref{sec:planners}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item Evidence that the Analyze layer, not the planner, decides whether closing the loop recovers production; that, given useful alarms, a capable LLM adds most as a veto over a statistical test; and that a fixed fault onset can manufacture conclusions that reverse under a sounder design (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

All code, data-processing scripts, prompts, every model decision with its stated reason, and all results are available (Section~\ref{sec:availability}).

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Autonomic computing and self-adaptive systems}
The MAPE-K reference model~\cite{kephart2003vision} structures a self-managing system as a feedback loop around a managed resource. Engineering such loops is the subject of the self-adaptive systems literature~\cite{weyns2020introduction}, which stresses that the Analyze phase must separate genuine change from noise before any adaptation is planned. LLMs have recently been examined as components of such loops: a state-of-the-art review and research roadmap organises their potential uses around the MAPE-K functions~\cite{li2024genai,li2024exploring}; LLM-based agents have been embedded in MAPE-K-based multi-agent systems~\cite{nascimento2023selfadaptive}; and MAPER extends MAPE-K with an LLM-based reasoning component to handle unanticipated runtime situations~\cite{alves2026maper}. Our study differs in placing an LLM inside the loop of an industrial system whose detector we first inherit unchanged and then replace, and in measuring it against tuned non-LLM baselines on held-out data.

\paragraph{Self-* properties in manufacturing}
Our earlier work integrated multi-agent systems, service orientation and autonomic computing for Industry~4.0 process coordination~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar,sanchez2020jii,sanchez2020survey}. The present paper re-examines that architecture empirically rather than proposing a new one.

\paragraph{Alarm management and change detection}
In process industries, alarm management standards require alarms to be rationalised, so that each one is meaningful and calls for a response, before operators are asked to act on them~\cite{isa182}. An autonomic controller that acts on alarms faces the same requirement, without an operator to absorb the noise. Detecting a change in the rate of an event stream or in the distribution of a measurement is a classical problem~\cite{basseville1993detection}; our rule-based planners and our per-station detector are simple instances of it.

\paragraph{LLMs and retrieval in industrial decision making}
RAG conditions a language model on documents retrieved for each query~\cite{lewis2020rag}. In manufacturing, LLM agents have been combined with digital twins to plan and control modular production~\cite{xia2023towards} and applied to advanced planning and scheduling~\cite{li2026a4ps}, and a recent review of LLMs for fault diagnosis in smart manufacturing covers, among other roles, retrieval-augmented fusion of industrial knowledge~\cite{thuan2026review}. Here, the retrieved knowledge is structured operational statistics computed by code from the controller's own history, not documents; we keep the term RAG for the retrieval step and state exactly what is retrieved (Section~\ref{sec:planners}).

\section{The original system revisited}
\label{sec:revisit}

\subsection{Simulator and data}
SSACSimulator is a Qt/C++ discrete-event simulator. A process model, derived from the Bosch Production Line Performance dataset~\cite{bosch2016kaggle}, describes 52 stations on four sub-lines plus begin and end nodes, 285 transitions (84 of them main flows), a throughput time of 6\,420~min and 1\,183\,165 cases. Four event traces of 100\,000 transitions each replay recorded piece movements; they span 54 to 168 hours of production. They are the traces prepared for the original study~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar}; we did not cut new ones. Production is recorded in batches: 62 of the 157 hours of trace 100m--200m contain no event.

\subsection{Headless port}
We ported the simulation core (about 1\,440 lines of C++ logic, excluding the graphical user interface) to Python. The port reproduces the original's behaviour as read from its source, including details that change results: thresholds computed in single precision, a 100\,000-event loading limit, and a lenient rule for malformed rows. A fast path advances only the pieces that can leave the system; the original per-tick loop is kept as a test oracle, and a regression test checks that both produce identical detections, decisions and piece lifetimes. Parity is with the original as read from its source, not with the compiled Qt binary. A run takes 0.2--0.4~s.

\subsection{What the Analyze layer actually does}
The original implements three detectors: a station whose mean transition time exceeds 1.3 times its reference; more than 10\% of stations flagged within one batch of simultaneous events; and a mean piece lifetime exceeding 1.1 times the throughput time. Only the second fires. The predicted-failure detector depends on a classifier label that is positive in 15 of 400\,000 events across all traces (0.004\%). The global detector is unreachable: the mean piece lifetime (636~min) is an order of magnitude below its threshold (7\,062~min). Finally, 45 of the 285 transitions have a reference time of zero, so any positive duration flags their station. Section~\ref{sec:legacy} shows what the one active detector sees once faults are injected.

Plan, Execute and Knowledge are absent. This is the starting point for the rest of the paper.

\subsection{Twin stations and the action space}
The Bosch line is built from parallel station pairs (and a few triples) that share predecessors and successors and split the load almost evenly; for example, on their main flows stations L0\_S2 and L0\_S3 receive 339\,345 and 334\,054 cases. We identify twins automatically from the overlap of their predecessor and successor sets on main flows: 29 of the 52 stations have one. Diverting a piece from a station to its twin keeps its trajectory consistent with the process graph. Structural equivalence does not guarantee equivalent equipment or capacity, which the anonymised dataset does not describe; a plant would have to confirm each twin. The action space is \textsc{Reroute}(S, T, $f$), which diverts a fraction $f$ of the pieces entering S to its twin T; \textsc{Restore}(S); \textsc{Maintenance}(S, $d$), which repairs S for $d$ minutes; and \textsc{NoOp}.

\subsection{Symbolic validator}
Every action, from any planner, is checked against the process graph and the current interventions before execution: stations must exist, a reroute target must be a structural twin, reroutes must not form chains, and a station cannot be repaired twice concurrently. The validator never judges whether an action is a good idea, only whether it is admissible.

\subsection{Effect model}
The simulation is trace-driven: start times come from the recorded data and are not shifted; faults and actions act on transition durations, and the lifetime of a piece is the sum of its transition durations. Delay therefore does not propagate as queueing; the model estimates time lost per piece. A Bosch ``transition'' is the time between two station timestamps and mostly measures waiting: its median is 0~min and its 90th percentile 18--30~min, but its 99th percentile reaches 4\,100--5\,900~min. Effects are therefore \emph{additive} and proportional to each station's processing time $\tau_S$ (the median of its transitions of up to 60~min), never multiplicative over a transition that includes days of storage. $\tau_S$ is 6 or 12~min for the eight stations where faults are injected, identical on all traces. The effect model is:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fault} of factor $k$ on S: $+(k(t)-1)\,\tau_S$ on every transition leaving S, where $k(t)$ is a \emph{step} ($k$ from $t_0$), \emph{intermittent} ($k$ for 60~min, 1 for 120~min, repeating) or \emph{drift} (rising linearly from 1 to $k$ over 600~min).
  \item \textbf{Reroute}: a deterministic fraction of pieces (selected by a hash of the case identifier) goes to T, which then adds $\alpha\,f\,\tau_T$ for the extra load.
  \item \textbf{Maintenance}: faults on S already active when the repair starts are cleared when it ends; a fault that begins during a repair is not. During the repair, in \emph{contingency} mode (default) backup capacity outside the model processes the pieces, adding $0.5\,\tau_S$; in \emph{stop} mode the pieces wait for the repair to finish. Repairing a healthy station costs and fixes nothing, which is what makes false alarms expensive.
\end{itemize}
Because the model is additive, the excess delay of a run decomposes exactly into the minutes added to individual transitions, which lets us measure it within any time window. The dataset is anonymised and provides no capacities, repair times or labelled failures, so all faults are synthetic and every parameter we could not ground in data (severity $k$, repair time $d$, congestion $\alpha$, maintenance mode) is varied in a sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:sweep}).

\subsection{A per-station Analyze on processing times}
\label{sec:perstation}
Section~\ref{sec:legacy} shows that the legacy detector reports the injected faults rarely and only inside line-wide bursts. Its station test compares means of whole transitions, dominated by hours or days of storage, so a fault that adds minutes of processing barely moves them. We therefore add an alternative Analyze layer that looks only at the processing regime: the transitions leaving a station that last at most 60~min. For each station, causally, it takes as reference the 75th percentile of those transitions over the previous 24~h and the rate $p_0$ at which they exceed it (Laplace-smoothed, because timestamps have a 6-min granularity); among the station's last $N$ processing transitions, it counts those above the reference and flags the station when that count is improbable under Binomial$(N, p_0)$ at level $\alpha$. Nothing is flagged during an 8-h warm-up; the stations flagged in one batch of simultaneous events are reported in a single alarm, and a station is not flagged again within a refractory period. $N$, $\alpha$ and the refractory period were chosen on the development trace (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}): $N=10$, $\alpha=10^{-3}$ and 6~min. With this layer, the legacy station alarms are no longer passed to the planners. The planners themselves are unchanged.

\section{Planners}
\label{sec:planners}

All planners receive, for each alarm, the alarm and the state of their own past interventions. None sees the injected faults. All of them respond to a station judged faulty in the same way: reroute to a free twin if one exists, then repair. Every planner, including the LLM planners, leaves a station it has acted on alone for 240~min.

\subsection{Rule-based planners}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Naive}: acts on every station named in an alarm.
  \item \textbf{Calibrated}: acts when the number of alarms on a station within a 120-min window is improbable (Poisson tail, $\alpha=10^{-3}$, at least 3 alarms) under its base rate, learned on a \emph{different} fault-free trace.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive}: the same test, against the rate observed in the preceding 24~h of the same trace, causally, after an 8~h warm-up.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive + load veto} (control): the adaptive rule, ignoring stations that receive load the controller itself rerouted onto them.
  \item \textbf{Twin-only} (control): acts on every flagged station that has a free twin and ignores the others.
\end{itemize}
The two controls are not proposed methods; they implement as plain rules behaviours observed in LLM planners, so that an LLM's contribution is measured beyond them. The default window, level and history above were fixed before any evaluation; we also re-tune them on the development trace (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\subsection{LLM planners}
For every alarm with actionable stations, the LLM must return, for each of them, a verdict (\emph{degraded}, \emph{normal}, \emph{self-inflicted} or \emph{insufficient evidence}) followed by an action type (none, repair, or reroute and repair). The output is constrained by a JSON schema whose station names are restricted to the actionable ones, so the decoder cannot invent a station. Code chooses the twin, discards any action inconsistent with the model's own verdict, and passes the rest to the validator.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1} receives the alarm, the actionable stations, whether each has a free twin, and the current interventions: the same structural information as the rules.
  \item \textbf{C2 (RAG)} additionally receives knowledge retrieved from the controller's operational memory: for each station, the alarms observed in the last 2 and 24~h against the number its fault-free base rate predicts, whether the controller is pushing rerouted load onto it, whether its alarms dropped after past repairs, and its most recent log records. Retrieval is keyed by station and ordered by recency; all statistics are computed by code.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid}: the adaptive change test proposes the stations; the LLM, with C2's knowledge, confirms or vetoes and chooses the action. A vetoed station is not re-examined for 120~min. The prompt tells the model that the test is usually right but cannot see context, and gives as examples load the controller rerouted onto a station, a past repair that did not reduce its alarms, and \emph{``a burst of alarms shared by many stations at once''}.
\end{itemize}
The system prompt describes how the Analyze layer raises alarms. Prompt version v2 describes the legacy detector; v3, used with the per-station detector, differs from v2 only in that paragraph. Both were fixed on the development trace before any held-out run.

\paragraph{Models}
We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2024}, 4-bit quantised, served locally with Ollama~\cite{ollama} at temperature~0; and Claude Fable~5.1 (Anthropic), accessed through the Claude Agent SDK~\cite{anthropic2026agentsdk} with effort set to \emph{low}, no tools and no external context sources. The frontier model does not expose a temperature setting, so its runs are not bit-reproducible. Every request and response of both models is stored, keyed by the full request. Identical requests are frequent, because before the fault onset a scenario yields the same states as the fault-free run of its trace, and they are served from this store: with the per-station detector, 2\,403 of the 26\,933 calls of C2 on Fable~5.1 were new. A new call takes a median of 5--8~s with either model, short against faults that cost hours. Each decision is a single sample of the model; we did not estimate run-to-run variability. Fable~5.1 was used through a Claude subscription, with model identifier \texttt{claude-fable-5-1}, between 6 and 15 October 2026. A first development pilot was discarded because the SDK passed the tool definitions of the account's connectors to the model; the harness was then restricted to no tools and its identifier added to the cache key, so that no response from that pilot was reused. The prompts are given in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Scenarios}
A fault of factor $k=3$ is injected on one of eight stations, four with a twin (L0\_S2, L0\_S4, L0\_S6, L3\_S35) and four without (L0\_S1, L0\_S8, L3\_S29, L3\_S33), all of which have observable processing times on every trace, with one of the three fault shapes, at one of three onsets per station and shape. Onsets are drawn uniformly between 24~h after the first event of the trace and 24~h before its last, from a generator seeded by the scenario's name. Each run stops 24~h after the onset. Each trace also has a fault-free run over its whole length. Repairs last 60~min, $\alpha=0.5$, and maintenance runs in contingency mode unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{Development and held-out traces}
The first trace (0--100m) is the development trace; the other three are held out (216 fault scenarios). On the development trace only, we chose the parameters of the per-station detector (27 configurations, selected by the recovery of the adaptive rule with load veto), re-tuned the rules (45 configurations of window, level and history for each adaptive rule, 15 of window and level for the calibrated rule), and fixed the prompts. Every prompt version tried and its results are logged in the repository. Calibrated rules and C2 learn their base rates on a fault-free trace other than the one they are evaluated on (100m--200m from the development trace; 200m--300m and 500m--600m from each other). During prompt development, the development runs took their base rates from 100m--200m, which is later held out; this exposes no fault labels.

\paragraph{Metrics}
Every run replays the same trace, so each piece is compared with itself in a fault-free reference run. The \emph{24-h recovery} of a planner over a set of scenarios is $1-\sum E_{24}(\text{planner}) / \sum E_{24}(\text{no action})$, where $E_{24}$ is the delay added to transitions in the 24~h after the fault onset: 1 erases the cost of the faults; 0 is no better than doing nothing; negative values mean that the planner's own actions cost more than the faults. A planner \emph{acts} on a scenario if it repairs the faulty station within those 24~h; \emph{false repairs} are repairs of other stations in the same window. The \emph{false-alarm cost} is the excess delay per hour in the fault-free runs.

\paragraph{Statistics}
The primary estimand is the difference in 24-h recovery between two planners, with a 95\% bootstrap interval~\cite{efron1993bootstrap} that resamples scenarios within their trace (10\,000 resamples). Because scenarios on the same trace share their alarm noise and three traces are few clusters, we also report a two-level bootstrap that resamples traces and then scenarios, and the difference with each trace left out. Secondary: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-scenario delay~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, which treats scenarios as independent and is therefore conditional on these traces, with Holm adjustment~\cite{holm1979} over the rows of each table block and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r$ as effect size. The breakdown by fault shape is exploratory.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The legacy Analyze layer gives too little evidence}
\label{sec:legacy}
The legacy station detector raises its alarms in bursts that follow the production batches rather than the faults. Without faults, on the held-out trace 100m--200m it raises 63, 18, 0, 0, 0, 2, 0 and 1 alarms in successive 12-h blocks; on 200m--300m, 66 in the first 12~h, none in the next 60~h, then 25, 18 and 16; on the development trace, 30, 9 and then none. Table~\ref{tab:detection} shows what it reports in the 24~h after a fault, with no planner. It names the faulty station in half of the held-out scenarios, 5.4 times on average against 1.2 in the same window without the fault, and each such alarm lists 6.5 stations on average, because the detector only fires when more than 10\% of the stations are flagged in one batch. It compares mean transition times that are dominated by storage, so a fault that adds 12~min of processing moves them little.

\subsection{A per-station Analyze layer}
\label{sec:sweep}
The per-station detector is noisier in absolute terms (Table~\ref{tab:detection}): it names a station that has no fault in half of the windows. But it reports the faulty station three times as often as the legacy detector, 15.1 alarms per scenario against 3.6 without the fault, and in alarms that list 2.3 stations on average, so a rate test on its alarms can single the station out. On the development trace, every configuration of the per-station detector names the faulty station within 24~h in all 72 development scenarios; the chosen one lets the adaptive rule with load veto recover $+0.84$ there. Table~\ref{tab:sweep} reports the rule-based planners with this detector on the held-out traces and a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis over all fault shapes. Acting on the detector's alarms pays off only when faults are severe relative to the cost of a repair and backup capacity absorbs the work during repairs. Halving the fault factor ($k=1.5$), quadrupling the repair time (240~min) or stopping the line during repairs makes every rule much worse than doing nothing, because the false repairs then cost more than the faults they fix; full congestion ($\alpha=1$) cancels the benefit. Shorter repairs, more severe faults or no congestion more than double it. The naive planner, which acts on every alarm, loses against doing nothing at the central point, and intermittent faults defeat every rule there. This analysis covers the rules only; we did not repeat it for the LLM planners.

\subsection{What the vetoes rest on}
The same hybrid on Fable~5.1 is the most conservative planner under the legacy detector and the best one under the per-station detector, with the same prompt. Its stated reasons barely change. Coded with an explicit keyword rule (\emph{burst}, \emph{line-wide}, \emph{common cause}, \emph{systemic}; rule and counts in the repository), 99\% of its vetoes argue that the alarms come in bursts shared by many stations under the legacy detector (1\,596 of the 1\,607 calls with a veto), and 91\% under the per-station detector (2\,587 of 2\,833); counted over distinct responses rather than calls, 97\% (119 of 123) and 87\% (249 of 285). Its vetoes of the faulty station cite this reason in 148 of 148 and 139 of 142 cases. The legacy detector raises an alarm \emph{only} when more than 10\% of the stations are flagged in the same batch, so under it every alarm is a shared burst and the argument vetoes nearly everything: the hybrid vetoed at least one station in 1\,607 of its 1\,812 calls. Under the per-station detector, a burst is one possible pattern among others, and the same argument separates line-wide disturbances from isolated faults: the hybrid vetoes in 2\,833 of 4\,348 calls and recovers twice as much as the rule. The hybrid's prompt names such bursts among the reasons to doubt the test; the 7B model, with the same prompt, uses this argument in fewer than 1\% of its vetoes under either detector. The stated reasons alone cannot show how the model weighs evidence, but the contrast between the two Analyze layers, with planner and prompt fixed, shows that what the alarms encode decides whether the veto helps or hurts.

\subsection{Why the evaluation design matters}
In a first phase of this research, every fault was injected 600~min after the first event of a trace, a repair cleared any fault that began during it, and recovery was measured over the whole trace. Under that design the legacy detector appeared to work: the adaptive rules recovered 52--54\%, C2 on Fable~5.1 58\% and the hybrid on Fable~5.1 24\% (Table~\ref{tab:designs}). Those gains were an artefact of the design. The fixed onset fell inside the detector's start-up burst of alarms and right after the 8-h warm-up of the planners' memory, when C2 repairs many stations at once; routine repairs then coincided with the fault or preceded it by minutes and, under the old repair rule, cleared it at no cost. Moving onsets out of the start-up period and letting repairs clear only active faults removed those gains and reversed the ranking of the two LLM designs. We report the first design here because the error is easy to make: a closed-loop controller evaluated at a single, fixed fault time inherits whatever happens to the line at that time.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The Analyze layer decides}
Closing the original loop~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar} on its own detector recovers nothing within a day, whatever the planner: no planner differs from doing nothing by more than 0.07, because the detector gives too little and too unspecific evidence about the faulty station. Replacing it with a per-station test on processing times, chosen on one development trace and kept fixed, moves the best rule from $-0.02$ to $+0.20$ and the best planner from $+0.07$ to $+0.41$. In MAPE-K terms, the information passed from Analyze to Plan is the first thing to get right.

\paragraph{Propose and veto}
Given useful alarms, the LLM adds most as a veto over a statistical test, not as a triage on its own. The test supplies a candidate only when a station's alarm rate departs from its own history; the model, with the retrieved statistics, rejects candidates that a line-wide disturbance, a reroute or an ineffective past repair explains. This division of labour keeps the decision anchored in a transparent test and uses the model where it has an advantage: weighing several pieces of context that a rule would have to enumerate. On their own, both LLM planners do worse than the rule; the frontier model without retrieved knowledge mostly declines to act.

\paragraph{Capability matters inside the right architecture}
The same hybrid recovers $+0.41$ on Fable~5.1 and $+0.29$ on the 7B model, which only ties the best re-tuned rule. This is a contrast between two systems, which differ in size, quantisation, provider and inference settings, not a measured capability curve.

\paragraph{Implications for practice}
Within the limits of a study with synthetic faults, four points transfer to automation architects and maintenance engineers. First, connecting threshold alarms directly to maintenance orders is harmful: the naive planner loses against doing nothing under both detectors, because false repairs accumulate; alarms must be rationalised before they drive actions, by analogy with what alarm-management practice requires for operators~\cite{isa182}. Second, a detector should compare each station with its own recent behaviour on the part of the signal a fault affects, here processing times rather than storage-dominated transitions. Third, a rate-change test is a cheap and transparent proposal layer and the reference any learned or LLM-based controller should be compared with. Fourth, an LLM can sit on top of such a test as an advisory veto; its non-determinism and its sensitivity to how alarms are presented argue for keeping every action behind a symbolic validator, as here, and, in a plant, behind human approval.

\paragraph{Development sets mislead}
The per-station detector and the re-tuned rules were chosen on the development trace, where the adaptive rule recovers $+0.84$; on the held-out traces it recovers $+0.20$, and the configurations re-tuned there help one rule and not the others. Under the first design, three conclusions drawn on the development trace were also wrong. A single trace is too small to select designs on, and LLM-based controllers should be evaluated on data not used to write their prompts, with fault onsets that do not coincide with the controller's start-up.

\paragraph{Threats to validity}
\emph{Construct}: faults are synthetic, and the effect model is additive and trace-driven, without queueing, so every recovery is a property of the model, not a measured property of the Bosch line. Its unverifiable parameters were swept one at a time for the rules (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}): mild faults, long repairs, full congestion and stop-mode maintenance remove or reverse the benefit of acting, so every recovery we report holds only for severe faults, short repairs and backup capacity during repairs. No sensitivity analysis was run for the LLM planners. Recovery is measured in production time within 24~h of the fault, without economic weights; the 24-h window can fall partly on hours without production, because the traces are recorded in batches. Twins are structural, not verified equipment equivalents. \emph{Internal}: each frontier-model decision is a single stored sample at low effort, and most calls reuse a stored response; the prompts are part of the treatment, and the hybrid's prompt contains the burst cue its vetoes cite; the control rules cover the load veto but not every statistic C2 retrieves, such as the outcome of past repairs; the 7B model is 4-bit quantised. The per-station detector and the evaluation design were introduced after results under the first design had been seen; both were fixed on the development trace, and the change is recorded in the repository before any held-out run with them. The frontier model may have seen our earlier papers~\cite{sanchez2020selfstar,sanchez2020jii}, which name the Bosch line, but the faults are synthetic and not described anywhere it could have seen. \emph{External}: one production line, one dataset; two language models, with no intermediate sizes. \emph{Statistical}: scenarios on the same trace are not independent; three held-out traces are few clusters, which the trace-level resampling addresses only partly.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We closed the autonomic loop of a self-coordinated manufacturing architecture that, as published, only detected. On its own detector, no planner recovers delay: the detector raises line-wide bursts of alarms and reports a faulty station rarely. With a per-station detector on processing times, a statistical change test recovers about a fifth of the delay within a day, and a frontier LLM with retrieved operational knowledge that vetoes the test's candidates recovers about two fifths, ahead of every rule configuration we tried. Used on its own, the same model does not beat the test. An earlier evaluation, with a fixed fault time, had suggested the opposite ranking. Future work will validate the effect model against plant data with real capacities and repair times, run the hybrid without the burst cue and with repeated samples, add intermediate model sizes, and extend the sensitivity analysis to the LLM planners.
